# Assistant

Althusser is defending Marx from a particular way of making Marxism into a philosophy of **human essence, alienation, and emancipation**—a philosophy in which capitalism is understood as the distortion of an already-given human nature and communism as its eventual restoration. He thinks that this interpretation is morally attractive, but theoretically misleading. It reads the mature Marx backwards through the conceptual vocabulary of the young Marx.

## 1. What Althusser means by the “young Marx”

The young Marx, especially in the *1844 Manuscripts*, still works within a broadly post-Hegelian and Feuerbachian problematic. His central terms include:

- species-being;
- human essence;
- alienation;
- objectification and dehumanization;
- the recovery of genuinely human social relations.

On this picture, human beings are essentially productive, social, and self-realizing. Under capitalism, their own activity confronts them as an alien power: their labor becomes a commodity, the products of labor dominate them, and social relations appear as relations between things. Communism would overcome this alienation and allow human powers to become transparent to themselves and collectively controlled.

Althusser does not deny that this is an important stage in Marx’s development. His claim is that it is not yet Marxist science in the mature sense. It remains organized by philosophical questions inherited from Hegel and Feuerbach: What is the human essence? How has it become alienated? How can it be reconciled with itself?

The decisive change, in Althusser’s account, occurs around 1845–46, in the *Theses on Feuerbach* and *The German Ideology*. Marx no longer begins from an abstract human essence and asks how it has been alienated. He begins from historically specific social relations, material production, forms of cooperation, property relations, and class conflict. That is what Althusser calls the “epistemological break”: the emergence of a new theoretical problematic, one that makes possible a science of history.

## 2. What is the problem with humanist Marxism?

Althusser’s objection is not primarily that humanist Marxists are insufficiently compassionate. It is that their interpretation tends to smuggle several philosophical assumptions into Marx’s scientific analysis.

### a. It treats “the human” as the explanatory starting point

For the humanist reading, capitalism is often explained by saying that it alienates the worker from his or her true humanity. But Althusser reverses the explanatory order. We should not explain capitalist relations by first positing a universal human essence. We should explain historically specific forms of “human life” through the relations in which people are materially situated.

In capitalism, people do not become workers because their human essence has been alienated. They become workers because they are separated from the means of production and must sell their labor-power in order to live. That is a social and historical relation, not primarily a psychological or anthropological condition.

### b. It turns exploitation into alienation

Alienation can describe an important experience of capitalist life, but it does not by itself explain exploitation. The central question in *Capital* is not simply why labor feels estranged or why human beings lose control over their products. It is how surplus value is extracted through the purchase and use of labor-power.

The worker can enter the labor contract formally as a free legal subject and still be exploited. The mechanism of exploitation is not adequately captured by saying that the worker is alienated from his or her essence. It depends on the structure of the wage relation, the distinction between labor-power and labor, and the production of surplus value.

For Althusser, if alienation becomes the master concept, the specific categories of *Capital*—commodity, value, labor-power, surplus value, capital, reproduction, and class struggle—risk being reduced to illustrations of a prior philosophical story about human self-loss.

### c. It makes communism the restoration of an original essence

Humanist readings can make history appear teleological: humanity begins in an authentic condition, becomes alienated under capitalism, and will finally return to itself in communism. Althusser regards this as a secularized version of a philosophical or religious narrative of fall and redemption.

Marx’s mature theory, on Althusser’s reading, does not require the claim that communism restores a pre-existing human essence. Communism is instead a new form of social organization produced through determinate historical contradictions and political struggle. It is not simply the recovery of something that capitalism had taken away.

### d. It privileges the subject over the structure

Humanist Marxism generally takes individuals, their needs, their consciousness, or their alienated activity as the basic units of analysis. Althusser argues that in *Capital* individuals appear within relations that they do not freely constitute: relations of property, production, exchange, class, and reproduction.

This does not mean that individuals are unreal or that they cannot act. It means that they are not the theoretical origin of the social structure. In capitalism, people often act as “bearers” of social relations: the capitalist functions as the personification of capital, while the worker functions as the bearer of labor-power. Their motives matter, but the structure of the relation cannot be explained merely by appealing to those motives.

## 3. Why reading *Capital* through the *1844 Manuscripts* can go wrong

Althusser thinks that such a reading produces several distortions.

### It makes *Capital* into an anthropology

Instead of reading *Capital* as an analysis of a historically specific mode of production, one reads it as a theory of what happens to human beings in general when their labor is appropriated by others. Capitalism then becomes a particularly intense example of a universal human problem rather than a determinate social system with a specific historical structure.

### It psychologizes or moralizes categories

Commodity fetishism, for example, can be interpreted as a form of human self-alienation or false consciousness. But Marx’s point is more objective and structural: relations among producers take the form of relations among things because production is organized through private labor and exchange. Fetishism is not simply a mistaken way individuals happen to perceive reality; it is generated by the social form itself.

Similarly, exploitation is not just an evil because it frustrates human fulfillment. It is a structural relation in which one class appropriates unpaid labor from another.

### It converts class struggle into a conflict between humanity and its alienation

For Althusser, classes are not merely groups of people occupying different positions in a moral drama of dehumanization. They are constituted through relations of production and antagonism. The question is not simply whether workers can regain control over their labor as human beings, but how the reproduction of capitalist relations can be interrupted and transformed through class struggle.

### It makes the mature theory depend on a philosophical foundation Marx had abandoned

If the *1844 Manuscripts* supply the real philosophical key to *Capital*, then value, surplus value, and capital become secondary expressions of alienation. Althusser thinks the direction should be reversed: the mature concepts should not be translated into the vocabulary of the early ones. The mature theory represents a break precisely because it no longer requires “human essence” as its foundation.

## 4. Is Althusser saying that Marx stopped caring about emancipation?

No. This is the crucial distinction between **theoretical anti-humanism** and hostility to human beings.

Althusser’s “anti-humanism” is a claim about the proper object and method of Marxist theory. It says that Marxism should not explain history by beginning with an abstract human subject or universal essence. It does not say that Marxism should abandon emancipation, equality, freedom, or the abolition of exploitation as political aims.

Indeed, Althusser thinks that abandoning the humanist vocabulary can make emancipation more concrete. If one explains suffering only as alienation from an essential humanity, one may fail to identify the mechanisms that reproduce it. A structural analysis can show what must actually be changed: property relations, the division of labor, the organization of production, the state, ideology, and the reproduction of class power.

His slogan might be put this way: **Marxism is for human emancipation, but it is not theoretically founded on a philosophy of Man.**

## 5. The political context matters

Althusser was also responding to the political and intellectual situation of the 1950s and 1960s. Humanist Marxism was in part a reaction against Stalinism and bureaucratic dogmatism. Appeals to alienation and human freedom could serve as a defense of Marxism against a dehumanizing communist state.

Althusser understood the political motivation. But he worried that this reaction replaced one simplification with another. Official Stalinism often reduced Marxism to economic laws and historical necessity; humanist revisionism could reduce it to an ethics of authentic existence and human fulfillment. Althusser wanted Marxism to be neither mechanical economism nor existential humanism, but a science of the complex structures and contradictions through which social formations reproduce themselves and change.

## 6. Was Althusser’s break too absolute?

Many readers think so, and Althusser himself later qualified his earlier formulation. Marx does not simply throw away every idea found in the early writings. Questions of domination, social power, fetishism, freedom, and the relation between human activity and social organization persist. But they are transformed by being placed within a different theoretical framework.

The *Grundrisse*, *Capital*, and the later writings also contain language about alienation, the reduction of workers to appendages of machinery, and the development of human capacities. So it would be too crude to say that mature Marx has no concern with human flourishing or that the young Marx is merely “un-Marxist.”

A more defensible formulation is that the early and mature writings ask overlapping questions with different explanatory priorities. The young Marx tends to ask what human beings are and how they become estranged from themselves. The mature Marx asks how historically specific social relations organize production, compel behavior, produce forms of consciousness, and reproduce class domination.

## The basic disagreement

The humanist says:

> Capitalism must be overcome because it alienates human beings from their essential powers; communism will restore their full humanity.

Althusser replies:

> That may express a compelling political aspiration, but it cannot serve as the theoretical key to *Capital*. Capitalism must be analyzed as a structured mode of production, and emancipation must be understood as the transformation of the relations that reproduce it—not simply as the recovery of an original human essence.

So Althusser is not defending Marx from concern with alienation or emancipation as such. He is defending him from the claim that those concerns, in their Feuerbachian and anthropological form, are the foundation of Marx’s mature theory. His fear is that reading *Capital* through the *1844 Manuscripts* turns a critique of political economy into a philosophy of alienation—and thereby obscures the specific social mechanisms that Marx spent his mature life trying to explain.

# Assistant

Yes—but only if “break” is understood as a **break in theoretical problematic**, not as a total rupture in Marx’s interests, vocabulary, or political commitments.

Althusser’s strongest claim is defensible: Marx’s mature analysis cannot simply be derived from the anthropology of the *1844 Manuscripts*. Around 1845–46, Marx increasingly stops asking, “What is the human essence, and how is it alienated?” and begins asking how historically specific relations of production organize social life. That is a real change in the *logic of explanation*.

The difference can be put schematically:

- **Young Marx:** human essence → alienation → emancipation.
- **Mature Marx:** social relations of production → class structure and reproduction → possibilities for transformation.

This matters because concepts such as labor-power, surplus value, capital, competition, and reproduction do not function merely as more precise names for alienation. They explain mechanisms that the language of alienation alone cannot adequately specify. A worker’s exploitation, for example, is not explained simply by the worker’s feeling estranged from the product or from self-realization. It is explained through the purchase and use of labor-power and the extraction of surplus value within a particular property relation.

So there is a break in at least three senses:

1. **A break with Feuerbachian anthropology:** Marx no longer treats an abstract “human essence” as the foundation of historical explanation.
2. **A break in the object of theory:** the focus shifts from humanity’s self-relation to historically determinate modes of production and their reproduction.
3. **A break in explanatory method:** social forms are explained through relations and structures rather than through the alienation of an already-defined subject.

But Althusser often makes this break too absolute. There are serious reasons to resist the idea that Marx simply leaves the problematic of alienation behind. The later Marx still analyzes workers’ subordination to capital, the domination of living labor by past labor, the reduction of human activity to an instrument of accumulation, and the conditions for the development of human capacities. The *Grundrisse* and *Capital* contain arguments that are difficult to understand without some concern for human powers and their domination.

Moreover, the young Marx was not merely a philosopher of inward authenticity. Even the *1844 Manuscripts* contain economic analysis, and “alienation” there concerns objective relations of labor, property, production, and social power—not only subjective feelings. Conversely, the mature Marx is not free of anthropological language. He continues to speak about human needs, capacities, freedom, social development, and the reduction of life to a means of accumulation.

The better conclusion, therefore, is that there is a **qualified break**:

- not a break between an apolitical young Marx and a scientific mature Marx;
- not a break between a humanist who cared about emancipation and a structuralist who did not;
- but a break between two different theoretical organizations of partly overlapping problems.

The young Marx tends to make alienation the organizing concept and political economy its evidence. The mature Marx makes the critique of political economy the organizing theory, within which domination, fetishism, human capacities, and emancipation are reinterpreted.

That distinction also explains why the break was so important to Althusser politically. He was defending Marx not from every appeal to freedom or human flourishing, but from the claim that Marxism must be grounded in a universal philosophy of Man. He thought that such a foundation made Marxism vulnerable to moralism, existentialism, and vague appeals to authenticity, while obscuring the mechanisms of exploitation and reproduction.

So: **yes, there is a break to defend—but not the sharp, clean, all-or-nothing break Althusser sometimes presents.** What should be defended is a change in Marx’s explanatory framework. What should be rejected is the stronger thesis that the early Marx is simply irrelevant to the mature one, or that mature Marxism has no legitimate concern with human emancipation. The most plausible reading is that the mature Marx does not discard the problem of alienation; he transforms and partially displaces it by embedding it in a theory of historically specific social relations.
