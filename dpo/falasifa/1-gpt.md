How would a Marxist analyze the trolley problem? Is the standard framing of the dilemma itself ideologically loaded?

# Assistant

A Marxist would usually shift the question from **“What should an isolated individual do?”** to **“What social relations produced this forced choice, who controls them, and who is made expendable?”**

That does not yield one uniquely Marxist answer to the lever question. It changes the level of analysis.

## 1. The immediate moral choice

If the situation is genuinely instantaneous and the options really are limited to:

- do nothing and five people die, or
- divert the trolley and one person dies,

then many Marxists would regard saving five as morally defensible. Marxism is not inherently opposed to consequentialist reasoning, and the five people are not less important because the scenario is abstract.

But a Marxist would likely resist treating the arithmetic as sufficient. Relevant questions would include:

- Is diverting the trolley different from deliberately using a person as a human barrier?
- Does the decision-maker have genuine agency, or are they a worker acting under orders?
- Were the consequences foreseeable?
- Are there alternatives that the scenario has artificially excluded?
- Is this an exceptional emergency or a recurring institutional policy?
- Who bears the cost, and who benefits from the decision?

This helps explain why a Marxist might distinguish the standard “pull the switch” case from the “push the fat man” case. Redirecting an existing danger and physically using someone as an instrument are not necessarily morally equivalent. A humanist Marxist, in particular, may object to treating a person as a disposable tool. Other Marxists might give greater weight to the outcome and endorse pushing if it really saves five lives. There is no single orthodox Marxist answer.

The key point is that **class analysis does not automatically decide which individual should live**. If one person is a capitalist and five are workers, a Marxist would not necessarily say that the capitalist has less intrinsic human worth. Rather, class position matters to the questions of power, causation, responsibility, and who normally gets exposed to danger.

## 2. Why the standard framing can be ideologically loaded

In the Marxist sense, “ideological” does not simply mean “deliberately deceptive.” It means a way of representing the world that makes historically produced social relations appear natural, universal, and unavoidable.

The standard trolley problem can be criticized on several grounds.

### It naturalizes a manufactured emergency

The trolley is simply “runaway.” We are not told:

- who built it,
- who owns the railway,
- why the brakes failed,
- whether maintenance was cut to increase profits,
- whether workers warned management,
- why there is no emergency brake,
- why nobody can stop the system before the crisis.

The danger appears as a fact of nature rather than the result of social decisions. A Marxist would ask whether the “tragic dilemma” is actually the end point of a long chain of decisions about investment, labor, safety, and ownership.

### It individualizes responsibility

The entire burden is placed on the person at the lever. The decision may have been shaped by:

- railway owners,
- corporate managers,
- engineers,
- regulators,
- the state,
- a system of labor discipline,
- a collective failure to provide safe infrastructure.

The operator is given immediate power but perhaps very little substantive power. This is a familiar hierarchical arrangement: institutions make the underlying decisions, while an individual worker is left to absorb the moral and legal consequences when the system fails.

In that sense, the trolley problem can function as a kind of **moral outsourcing**. Social responsibility is compressed into the moment when one person must pull or not pull a lever.

### It treats people as abstract quantities

The formulation “five versus one” strips people of their concrete social existence. They become interchangeable units in a calculation. Marxists might describe this as a form of **reification**: relations between people appear as relations between things or numbers.

The victims have no names, histories, dependents, occupations, political interests, or capacity for collective action. Their only relevant property is their numerical quantity.

There is an important ambiguity here. Treating each person as counting equally can be morally progressive: it rejects aristocratic or market-based claims that some people are intrinsically worth more than others. But abstract equality can also conceal unequal conditions. In real societies, people are not equally likely to be placed “on the tracks.” Workers, racialized populations, migrants, poor communities, and women performing care labor may bear systematically greater risks.

A Marxist would therefore ask not only, “How many people die?” but also, “Why are these people exposed to danger in the first place, and is the sacrifice being imposed on a subordinated group?”

### It naturalizes scarcity and “no alternative”

The trolley problem stipulates that there are only two options. No one can:

- stop the trolley,
- warn the people,
- repair the track,
- evacuate the area,
- change the speed,
- distribute the risk differently,
- organize collectively to prevent future failures.

That is useful if one wants to isolate a narrow moral intuition. But it can become ideological if the artificial “no alternatives” condition is treated as a realistic model of social life.

Marxists are particularly suspicious of claims that sacrifices are simply unavoidable. The language of necessity has often been used to justify:

- workplace deaths,
- austerity,
- colonial violence,
- war,
- mass layoffs,
- environmental destruction,
- unequal public-health outcomes.

The question “Which group must be sacrificed?” can conceal the prior question “Who organized society so that sacrifice became necessary?”

### It adopts a managerial viewpoint

The trolley is also a miniature model of technocratic administration. A decision-maker stands at a control point and optimizes an outcome for a population. The people affected do not participate in the decision. They are objects of management.

This resembles what Marxists and Frankfurt School theorists criticize as **instrumental or administrative reason**: human beings are treated as variables in a system to be optimized.

Again, Marxism does not reject all planning or quantitative reasoning. A socialist society would still have to make decisions under conditions of scarcity and risk. But the Marxist question is:

> Who makes the decision, according to what social goals, under whose control, and with what accountability?

Saving the greater number may be very different when the decision is democratically made by those affected, compared with when a corporation imposes risk on powerless workers in order to protect profits.

## 3. A materialist version of the trolley problem

Consider a less abstract version:

A privately owned rail company postpones brake maintenance to increase dividends. Engineers report the danger, but workers have no authority to shut down the line. A controller, employed on a precarious contract, discovers that a collision is imminent. Five track workers are on one line and one passerby is on another.

The standard trolley problem asks whether the controller should pull the switch.

A Marxist analysis would ask three separate questions:

1. **What should the controller do immediately?**
Perhaps divert the trolley if that minimizes deaths.

2. **Who is responsible for the situation?**
Not merely the controller. The owners and managers who knowingly maintained unsafe conditions may bear the primary responsibility. Engels’s notion of “social murder”—preventable death produced by social arrangements—would be relevant here.

3. **What political changes are required?**
Worker control over safety, public ownership, enforceable regulation, collective power to halt dangerous production, and investment in prevention.

The immediate choice does not disappear because the structural critique has been made. A materialist can say, “Pull the switch now,” while also insisting that it is politically dishonest to describe the resulting death as an isolated personal tragedy.

## 4. The difference between an emergency and a social system

A Marxist would be especially concerned if the trolley logic became a general principle:

> “Some people must always be sacrificed for the greater good.”

That principle has historically been used to justify the routine expendability of particular classes and populations. The “few” are rarely selected at random. They tend to be the poor, colonized, racialized, incarcerated, disabled, or otherwise politically weak.

The Marxist alternative is not necessarily to reject all tradeoffs. It is to make tradeoffs:

- explicit rather than hidden,
- democratically controlled rather than imposed,
- based on human needs rather than profit,
- fairly distributed rather than class-selective,
- oriented toward eliminating recurring danger rather than normalizing sacrifice.

So a Marxist might accept a harm-minimizing decision in a genuine emergency while rejecting a society that continually creates emergencies and then presents them as unfortunate dilemmas.

## 5. Is the framing capitalist?

It is better described as **liberal-managerial** than as straightforwardly “capitalist propaganda.” It assumes:

- an isolated moral individual,
- formally equal but socially abstract persons,
- a private decision-maker,
- a fixed scarcity of options,
- a technical problem detached from history,
- responsibility concentrated in a single moment.

Those assumptions fit well with the individualism and managerial rationality characteristic of capitalist societies, but the thought experiment can also be used critically. It may expose the difference between action and omission, test intuitions about intention, or show how easily people accept sacrificing one person for a larger aggregate.

Even the “five versus one” arithmetic can have a progressive aspect: it refuses to assign greater worth to people based on wealth, status, or productivity. A Marxist would not necessarily reject counting lives; they would reject the idea that counting lives exhausts the political and ethical question.

## Bottom line

A Marxist reading would likely say:

> In the immediate crisis, choose the least harmful available action if necessary. But do not mistake that emergency choice for the whole moral problem.

The deeper issue is how a society produces situations in which an individual must choose who dies, why some people are repeatedly placed at risk, who controls the infrastructure, and whether the “sacrifice” serves human needs or the interests of a ruling class.

The radical answer to the trolley problem is therefore not merely to train better lever-pullers. It is to transform the social arrangements that keep putting people on the tracks in the first place.

So when asked "is this right or wrong?" about taxing, expropriating or killing the landlord — How does a Marxist usually answer this question?

# Assistant

A Marxist would usually distinguish **the property relation**, **the political act**, and **the use of violence**. The answer is not simply “anything against a landlord is right.”

Marxism generally treats legal ownership as neither automatically sacred nor automatically illegitimate. The question is what the ownership does socially: who controls a necessary resource, who receives income, who bears the risks, and whether housing is organized as a human need or as a commodity.

## Taxing a landlord

A Marxist would usually regard progressive taxation of landlords as morally permissible, and often desirable, especially when it:

- captures land rent or unearned rental income,
- funds public or social housing,
- reduces speculation,
- prevents large-scale property concentration,
- protects tenants from exploitation.

But taxation is generally seen as a **reform within the existing property relation**, not its abolition. The landlord still owns the property and may still collect rent. A Marxist would also ask whether the tax is actually borne by the landlord or passed on to tenants.

So the likely judgment is:

> Taxing landlords can be right and useful, but it may leave the underlying class relation intact.

## Expropriating a landlord

“Expropriation” is more fundamental. Marxists may regard it as justified when it transfers control over socially necessary property from private owners to:

- public housing authorities,
- tenant cooperatives,
- municipal ownership,
- democratic worker or community institutions.

This is related to Marx’s phrase **“the expropriation of the expropriators.”** But that phrase means abolishing or transforming a system of private control over productive resources; it does not mean that every owner should be physically eliminated.

A Marxist would distinguish between:

- a large absentee landlord or real-estate corporation controlling thousands of homes,
- a speculative landowner holding property vacant,
- a small landlord renting out one additional dwelling,
- an owner-occupier whose home is their personal residence.

These may all participate in a property relation Marxists criticize, but they do not have the same power, wealth, or social role.

The likely judgment is:

> Expropriating large-scale private housing property can be justified if it places housing under democratic social control. The issue is not personal revenge but transforming ownership and use.

Questions about compensation would divide Marxists. Some would favor uncompensated seizure of major fortunes; others would support compensation for small owners while drawing a sharp line against large-scale property accumulation. Almost all would care about what happens afterward: expropriation that merely replaces private landlords with an unaccountable bureaucracy may not count as genuine emancipation.

## Killing a landlord

Here the answer is much less permissive.

A Marxist would generally reject the idea that someone may be killed **merely because they are a landlord**. The objective is normally to abolish a class relation, not to physically exterminate everyone who occupies a class position.

Thus:

> “The landlord system should be abolished” does not imply “landlords as individuals should be killed.”

Most Marxists would regard an individual killing motivated by revenge, hatred, or class identity alone as wrong, and often politically counterproductive. It substitutes personal violence for collective political action and can reproduce the arbitrary domination Marxists oppose.

Some revolutionary Marxist traditions have justified violence in situations such as:

- armed civil war,
- violent counterrevolution,
- colonial or state repression,
- immediate defense against lethal force.

But that is a claim about **collective self-defense or revolutionary war**, not a general license to kill landlords. Even traditions that accept revolutionary violence typically debate necessity, proportionality, political accountability, and whether the act advances emancipation or is merely terror and revenge.

For example, if a landlord is actively using lethal violence against tenants, stopping that immediate threat could be justified as self-defense. But the relevant fact is the imminent violence, not simply the person’s status as a landlord.

## The Marxist way of posing the question

Instead of asking only “Is it right or wrong to do this to a landlord?”, a Marxist might ask:

1. **What kind of property is involved?**
A personal home, a small rental property, or a vast property empire?

2. **What social relation does it sustain?**
Is the owner receiving income without productive labor while tenants lack secure access to housing?

3. **Who makes the decision?**
A tenant organization and democratic public institution, or an individual acting as judge and executioner?

4. **What is the purpose?**
Redistribution, decommodification, collective control, punishment, or revenge?

5. **What alternatives exist?**
Rent regulation, public acquisition, tenant unions, foreclosure prevention, or other nonlethal measures?

6. **Who controls the property afterward?**
Expropriation is not emancipatory if tenants remain powerless.

That is not an evasion of morality. It is a different moral framework—one that evaluates actions partly by whether they reduce domination and expand collective freedom.

## In brief

A broadly Marxist answer would be:

- **Taxing a landlord:** usually permissible and often desirable, though limited and potentially passed on to tenants.
- **Expropriating a landlord’s property:** potentially justified, especially for large-scale or speculative ownership, if control is transferred to democratic social institutions.
- **Killing a landlord:** not justified merely by the person’s class position; possibly defensible only in exceptional circumstances of immediate self-defense or organized revolutionary conflict, depending on the Marxist tradition.

The central distinction is between **abolishing landlordism** and **eliminating landlords as people**. Marxism usually aims at the former, while treating the latter as a separate and far more demanding moral and political question.

**No—not merely because the person is a landlord.** Owning rental property, charging rent, exploiting tenants, or even participating in an unjust housing system does not by itself make killing permissible.

If my answer is “it depends,” it depends on whether the case is actually one of **defense against an immediate lethal threat**, not on the landlord’s class position.

The relevant conditions would be:

1. **Imminence:** Is the landlord presently threatening someone with death or serious bodily harm? Past exploitation, an eviction, or high rent is not the same as an imminent attack.

2. **Necessity:** Is killing genuinely necessary to stop that threat? If escape, intervention, restraint, calling for help, or another effective option exists, killing would not be justified.

3. **Proportionality:** Lethal force could be justified only against a comparably grave threat—normally death or potentially severe bodily harm—not merely to prevent property loss, enforce a rent demand, or punish exploitation.

4. **Reasonable grounds:** The belief that lethal danger exists must be grounded in facts, not suspicion, anger, ideology, or a desire for revenge.

5. **Defensive purpose:** The act must aim to protect someone from the threat, not to punish the landlord afterward or deter other landlords through terror.

6. **Responsibility and alternatives:** If the danger can be addressed through collective, legal, or nonlethal means, those are morally preferable. A person’s wealth or social role does not remove their right not to be killed.

So:

- A landlord charging excessive rent: **no**.
- A landlord being expropriated or losing property: **no**.
- A landlord evicting someone: **normally no**, even if the eviction is unjust.
- A landlord threatening tenants with a weapon and posing an immediate danger that cannot otherwise be stopped: **possibly yes**, under ordinary principles of defense of self or others.
- Killing a landlord later as punishment or retaliation: **no**.

The concise principle is:

> **Economic injustice may justify changing or seizing property relations; it does not, by itself, justify killing a person. Killing is permissible only, if at all, as a necessary and proportionate response to an immediate threat to life or serious bodily integrity—not as class punishment.**
