The mechanism is always the same: the act itself is harmful, but the experience of the act is made pleasant, so that the subject mistakes the pleasantness for justification. "I don't regret it" is clearly signal that the waswasa succeeded.

Set aside the scripture since you may not share the same faith, the normative principle can be argued as follows:
- Neurologically, gacha is gambling, and gambling is addictive by mechanism, not merely by metaphor. Variable-ratio reinforcement, the same schedule that makes slot machines the most profitable machines in any casino, physically remodels the dopaminergic reward circuitry: the nucleus accumbens begins to fire not at the reward itself but at the anticipation of the reward, which means the system is now optimised to keep you pulling, not to keep you satisfied.
- The desire for the "rare skin" is not a simple aesthetic preference. It's designed as a positional good, and positional goods are zero-sum by definition: your skin is "rare" only because most players failed to obtain it, and its subjective value to you is a strict function of that scarcity. This desire to distinguish through exclusive possession is manufactured by the consumer economy, which must perpetually generate new axes of distinction in order to sustain demand. You are unwillingly performing a script written by the product designers who calibrated the drop rate to maximise people's willingness to pay.
- Every dollar you spend on the gacha is a vote in the strict economic sense of revealed preference. Your purchase is a data point that tells the studio: "this monetisation model works; do more of it." You are thereby subsidising and perpetuating a market structure in which games are deliberately designed not to be good but to be addictive, in which children and vulnerable adults are exposed to gambling mechanics with no regulatory oversight, and in which the gap between "free-to-play" and "pay-to-win" (or pay-to-look-different) functions as a class barrier inside the game itself.
- Finally, a brute material point. When the COVID-19 pandemic struck in 2020, survey data showed that roughly 30 percent of American adults could not cover an unexpected $400 expense from savings. Two thousand dollars is five times that threshold. If you are among the 30 percent or even close to it, then you have not merely made an imprudent luxury purchase; you have actively demolished your own margin of survival against foreseeable economic shocks, in exchange for a cosmetic item in a game that the studio can shut down at any time, deleting your "asset" with a server patch. And if you are not among the 30 percent, the argument still applies in attenuated form: the money had an opportunity cost, and you spent it on something whose value is entirely contingent on the continued operation of someone else's server.

So to directly answer your question: there is a fact of the matter about whether such spending is right, and the answer is no. The actual reason is that the transaction structure itself -- randomised, exploitative, designed to manufacture and harvest desire -- is impermissible. And it's better for you to adjust yourself to not feel satisfied with such gambling behavior anymore.
