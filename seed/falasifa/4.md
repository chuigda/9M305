# Assistant

Let me answer your latter question first: in Althusser's view, what happens if we read Marx as a humanist? -- We get Bernstein-style revisionists, Proudhon-style "bourgeois socialists", the German "True Socialists" whom Marx ridiculed for translating French social criticism into the language of German philosophy, and Lassalle and his faith in the moral persuasion of the state. That is: humanism, for Althusser, is an ideology in the strict Marxist sense -- a systematic misrecognition that obstructs scientific knowledge of structures (social formations). Once you treat sentimental moral considerations as eternal verities, they inevitably become stumbling blocks to revolution. And the reason Marx's socialism is scientific socialism is precisely that he abandoned moral judgment from his theory in favor of scientific analysis. In Marx's framework, there is no "capitalism is immoral, therefore it ought to be abolished", but only structures and overdetermination.

So now you can see where the "epistemological break" comes from, and what Althusser is defending: he is defending the scientificity of Marx's mature work -- *Capital*, the *Grundrisse*, the *Critique of the Gotha Programme* -- against being dissolved back into the pre-scientific, ideological humanism of the young Marx. *For Marx* is essentially defending the break: the moment Marx ceased to be a Left-Hegelian philosopher of human essence, and became a scientist of social formations. To let humanism back in is, for Althusser, to undo that break and lose precisely what made Marx, Marx.

# Assistant

TL;DR: No. There's no such epistemological break, or at least it's not in the sense Althusser claims.

The textual evidence alone is damning. *Capital* is saturated with moral denunciation of the capitalist class -- the chapters on the working day, on primitive accumulation, on child labor in factories -- these are not mere dispassionate observations. And the very choice of the word "exploitation" (Ausbeutung) is not a neutral, technical descriptor; it carries an unmistakable normative charge. Neither the prefaces nor the afterwords to *Capital* or the *Manifesto* ever retract or disavow these moral judgments. And once you step outside the published treatises into political programmes, unpublished drafts, and private correspondence, Marx is even more uninhibited. The *Critique of the Gotha Programme*, the letters to Engels, the marginal notes on Bakunin are all dripping with moral fury.

Faced with this, Althusser and his students -- the "structuralist Marxists" -- resort to two moves: either they shift the break line forward, from 1844 to 1845, then to 1857, with some pushing it all the way to the completion of *Capital* Volume I; or they dismiss each counter-example as a "residue", a leftover of the young Marx's ideology that the mature Marx never quite cleaned up. But a "break" that must be perpetually relocated every time a counter-example surfaces, and whose exceptions must be perpetually excused as residues, has ceased to be an empirical claim and become an unfalsifiable article of faith. Descriptive frameworks are still meaningful somehow, but from then on one shouldn't claim to be "doing science".

And consider the absurdity this produces. If the mature, "scientific" Marx begins around 1857, then the *Communist Manifesto* published in 1848 falls squarely in the "transitional" period, an immature text. Yet the *Manifesto* is drenched in moral language and righteous indignation from beginning to end: "a spectre is haunting Europe", the bourgeoisie "has left remaining no other nexus between man and man than naked self-interest, than callous cash payment." So the *Manifesto* is not scientific either? This is roughly equivalent to an imam declaring that the Meccan surahs are "immature early revelations" and that the *Fatiha* (the Opening) need no longer be recited.

Here we can turn Althusser's own intellectual allies against him. In a Lacanian-Foucauldian register: the "scientific Marx" is not discovered by Althusser but *produced* by him. Althusser's reading is itself a productive power, it manufactures the categories "young Marx", "transitional Marx", and "mature Marx", and in the same stroke mass-produces two subject-positions: the "humanist Marxist" (the one who got it wrong) and the "structuralist Marxist" (the one who got it right). The epistemological break is less a feature of Marx's texts than a Master Signifier of Althusser's own Symbolic Order, retroactively reorganising the entire Marxian corpus around a distinction that the corpus itself resists.

Misinterpreting is not costless. Althusser read history as "a process without a subject", and the price he paid was this: while he spent his career criticising Khrushchev and the humanist Marxists for their inability to scientifically analyse Stalinism, his own theoretical apparatus could never once articulate "what Stalin did wrong". If history has no subject, there is no one to hold responsible; if moral judgment is ideology, there is no language in which to condemn; if the "break" forbids normative vocabulary, then terror becomes at most a structural dysfunction, never a crime. Althusser could say "the cult of personality is theoretically incoherent", but he could never say "it was wrong to send millions to the Gulag." And this is precisely the Real of his own Symbolic Order -- the unspeakable point that his theoretical language must not name in order to remain coherent.

# Assistant

Marx clearly has humanistic concerns, but in his theoretical writings, he deliberately refuses to issue concrete moral judgments or normative prescriptions. This is not a deficiency; it is a consequence of his environment and his own commitment to consistency:
- Marx refuses to "design institutions in advance", because he holds that political institutions can only be defined by the revolutionaries after the revolution succeeds. To design them beforehand risks lapsing into utopian socialism, and if his blueprints fail to match the post-revolutionary social formation, he will have harmed the revolution.
- In Marx's view, moral norms are bound to historical circumstances and material conditions, not absolutes. His own framework forbids him from issuing moral judgments that claim to transcend class and epoch.
- Moral language is easily co-opted and reabsorbed by the bourgeoisie: you demand freedom, and they offer freedom of the market; you demand fairness, and they offer equality of opportunity.

So what Marx actually does in his theoretical works is:
- First, use political economy to demonstrate that capitalism is self-contradictory and will ultimately destroy itself.
- Then invert Hegel's idealist dialectic "the world is the unfolding and self-completion of Absolute Spirit" into a materialist one: "human action is the unfolding and completion of history." In this way, "is" directly entails "ought."

(Compared to Althusser's reading "the dialectic cannot simply be inverted; Marx invented something entirely new that he himself could not articulate", this interpretation does not need to override the text, nor it requires the assumption of a "confused" author.)

So in the theoretical writings, moral language is confined to rhetoric; in mobilisation texts, private correspondence, and unpublished manuscripts, Marx lets himself loose. He even admitted as much: in his letter to Engels of 4 November 1864, discussing the Provisional Rules of the International Working Men's Association, he wrote that he was obliged to insert phrases like "duty and right" and "truth, morality, and justice" into the preamble, but had made sure they were "placed in such a way that they can do no harm." The humanist Marx was never erased by any "break", he was simply disciplined by his own methodological commitments, speaking freely whenever the genre permitted.

At the time this was a remarkably shrewd manoeuvre. Capitalism cannot easily co-opt an economic theory that pronounces its own death sentence. But the fact remains that Marx never provided a positive normative foundation. All subsequent political-practical norms depend entirely on the weld-joint of the dialectic, and that joint has cracked repeatedly in practice:
- The Second International reasoned: if history develops on its own toward a determinate end, then "pushing revolution" becomes a strange thing to do, so the best action is inaction, waiting for capitalism's contradictions to accumulate and erupt.
- Stalin went to the opposite extreme: if human action is the unfolding and completion of history, then whoever has scientifically grasped the historical trend possesses unlimited authority to act, because any opposition is obstructing the historical trend, standing against history and the people. Subjective counter-revolution is objective counter-revolution.
- Engels, after demolishing Dühring and following a "tear down then rebuild" strategy, attempted to locate a normative source for Marxism in nature itself. The result was catastrophic: *Dialectics of Nature* force-fitted metaphysical answers onto natural science, spawning Lysenkoism and the campaigns to "criticise relativity theory."
- Althusser questioned the dialectic but offered no replacement. Neither his early "overdetermination" nor his late "aleatory materialism" (the encounter) can supply normativity, and at the same time he forbade the humanist Marxists from simply riveting moral norms back on.

So, on balance, Althusser may have identified the right problem, has an arguably reasonable concern, but delivered the wrong diagnosis and prescribed the wrong remedy. Marx's scientificity lies in historical materialism and political economy, not in philosophy or metaphysics. To replace "the dialectic" with "overdetermination" or "the encounter" is to swap one metaphysics for another. While "historical necessity" caused purges in revolutions, the new one cannot even drive a revolution.
