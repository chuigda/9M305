# User

How would a Marxist analyze the trolley problem? Is the standard framing of the dilemma itself ideologically loaded?

---

# Assistant

The classical Marxists usually deflect the problem away with the famous quote "The human essence is the ensemble of social relations", i.e., they'll say the problem isn't whether to pull the lever, but who's actually on the track, who built the trolley and who designed the lever -- mental experiments abstracting away the social relations won't work.

I'd partially agree with them: the real society is not as simple as the "1-vs-5" binary choice. But there are actually isomorphic cases, and even just in their reforms and revolutions:
- The landlord: This land was purchased by my grandparents, and it's mine according to the heritage law.
- The peasants: We cannot afford the land tax, our children are starving to death.

Here the social relations of everybody (and their material interests) is now certain, and the measures you can take now constitute a continuum instead of a binary choice, yet:
- Should we collect taxes from the landlords from their income?
- Should we collect taxes based on property instead of income?
- Should we expropriate the landlords by force and redistribute the land to the peasants?
- And are we allowed to kill the landlord due to ... *some reason*?

See? The question "is this right or wrong?" survives even after we fill-in every detail.

So when asked "is this right or wrong?" about taxing, expropriating or killing the landlord — How does a Marxist usually answer this question?

---

# Assistant

On this question, classical Marxists usually use the dialectical method. Their argument (in my own simplified form) basically looks like:
- 1. [economic analysis] The economic exploitation is self-contradictory, and the contradiction is the drive pushing the world forward by destroying the exploitation and creating a new social form.
- 2. [inverted Hegel dialectics] We're not observing outside but operating inside the society, "Philosophers have hitherto only interpreted the world in various ways; the point is to change it." Our movements will be the expansion and accomplishment of the history.
- 3. The interest of the working class is the course of history. Thus, we should stand with the working class.

So basically, it's kind of "power of history (which the working class 'is standing along with') decides". They textually granted the power a non-divine, non-a-priori authority -- history necessity / history course.

Althusser would give a different interpretation for step 2: Marx didn't just invert Hegel's dialectics but actually invented something completely new which could not fit in Marx's philosophical language. He'd argue, via symptomatic reading, that *Das Kapital* actually describes over-determination, and history is a process without a subject (and even without destination).

Analytical Marxists (like G.A. Cohen) would agree with neither the classical nor Althusser. They identify the dialectics invocation as an ill-formed rhetoric-as-argument, and Marxism is normatively deficient. So a rigorous normative foundation should be established otherwise, explicitly answering "should or should not".
